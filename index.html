Why ten blocks a second is hard.

Speed needs blocks often. But a new block takes a fraction of a second to reach every miner, and two found in that window collide. On a single chain one is thrown away, with the work behind it.

Bitcoin avoids collisions by making blocks rare: one every ten minutes. Kaspa keeps both blocks, in a .

The collision maths

Blocks arrive at random, like raindrops: a Poisson process with rate λ. Two collide when the next one is found before the first has spread, which takes delay d. The share a chain discards grows with λ × d. At Kaspa’s ten a second and a 250 ms delay, λd = 2.5 and a chain would discard about 72% of blocks. Checked against the PHANTOM/GHOSTDAG paper, eprint.iacr.org/2018/104.

“Maths” mode also shows the network-delay slider in the toy above.

For you: on one chain, fast blocks waste work and that weakens security. Kaspa keeps every block, so it can be fast without giving security up.

How the order is built

Each block names one selected parent, the parent with the most blue work behind it. Following those links gives a spine through the web, the selected chain (brighter squares). Each chain block merges the blocks it saw that its selected parent had not: its mergeset. The order is: everything before, then the mergeset sorted by accumulated work, then the chain block itself.

This page rebuilds that order in your browser from fields the node publishes for every block: the chain flag, selected parent, mergeset and blue work. A node’s own virtual-chain call is the authority; this is a view of it. Blocks the network hasn’t merged yet are shown as waiting.

A block stays blue if, among the blocks it could not have seen, at most k are blue. Kaspa sets k from the block rate and a worst-case network delay. Mainnet runs GHOSTDAG; DAGKnight is the planned successor that adapts to the network instead (chapter 4).

Payments in parallel blocks don’t queue.

separate coins (Kaspa)one balance per account

Kaspa tracks money as separate coins, the way Bitcoin does, rather than one balance per account. Payments that land in parallel blocks don’t have to wait for each other.

For you: every node lines up the same blocks the same way, so everyone agrees on who paid whom, ten times a second.

Why the hidden blocks turn red

While you hid, the honest network kept making blocks your branch never saw. That gap is your blocks’ anticone. A block stays blue only if at most k blue blocks sit in its anticone. Honest blocks, published at once, have small anticones. Yours has a huge one, so it’s coloured red and ordered after the honest ones. Red blocks still count; they just can’t jump the queue.

Where “1 to 3 seconds” comes from

For Kaspa, Sompolinsky gives figures on X (26 Feb 2026). At 10 blocks a second, a 37% attacker can fake the majority signal within a second with probability 12%. At 100 blocks a second that drops to 0.3%. He also writes that Bitcoin reaches censorship resistance in about an hour and Kaspa reaches the same level in 1 to 3 seconds. Post, post. That’s why core wants more blocks per second, not for throughput. “Scalability is boring,” in his words.

When the internet breaks

GHOSTDAG fixes its safety margin k in advance, from an assumed worst-case delay. If the real network is much slower, a fixed margin can be too tight. , Kaspa’s planned next consensus, measures the network as it goes: fast when the internet is calm, slower but still safe when it splits. Core developers merged more DAGKnight code toward testnet in September 2026; it isn’t on mainnet.

For you: a payment you can treat as final before you’ve put your phone away. The rest of the page is about keeping it that way.

No premine. No sale.
No insiders.

On 7 November 2021 Kaspa started with an empty ledger. Every coin since was paid to whoever mined a block, on the same terms for everyone.

The company that funded early research, DAGLabs, mined an estimated 2.5 to 3% of supply in the first months, in the open, like any other miner.

Where these numbers come from

Kaspa: the node’s genesis file ships an empty coin set. Ethereum: about 12M of the ~72M genesis ETH (16.7%) went to founders, early contributors and the Ethereum Foundation; the rest was sold in the 2014 crowdsale. Cardano: IOHK, Emurgo and the Cardano Foundation held 11.52% of the 45B maximum. XRP: all 100B were created for Ripple and its founders.

Most of it is already out

No cliffs

What’s left trickles out slowly. The reward halves every year in twelve monthly steps of about 5.6%, instead of Bitcoin’s sudden halvings. The last coins come out around 2057.

Who pays the miners later

Today new coins pay almost all of it. As they taper, fees have to grow. It’s the most common serious doubt about Kaspa, and a fair one.

For you: no one can dilute your coins, and the open question is the one on the chart. Will fees grow enough to pay miners once new coins run low?

Is it an investment?

It’s a bet on use. There’s no company, no earnings and no dividend. The price moves only with what people will pay for the coin, which depends on whether Kaspa gets used.

  • It swings hard. KAS is 84% below its July 2024 high, and fell 9% in the last day.
  • It can go to zero. Most coins ever launched are now worth close to nothing.
  • Fees must take over. Security is paid by new coins today. If fees don’t grow, miners leave and security drops (chapter 6).
  • Competition. Ethereum, Solana and others are larger and already host most apps.
  • Your keys, your problem. Lose the seed words and the coins are gone. Exchanges can fail or freeze withdrawals.
  • Rules can change. Taxes and exchange rules for crypto differ by country and still shift.

This page explains; it doesn’t recommend. Only put in what you could lose entirely.

How covenants work

Toccata turned on four proposals. KIP-17 lets a script check the transaction spending it, so a coin can demand a matching next output. KIP-20 gives covenants an ID that consensus tracks, so lookalikes can’t be forged. KIP-16 lets a script check a zero-knowledge proof instead of re-running the work. KIP-21 adds sequencing commitments so an app can prove its own lane. Activation was at DAA score 474,165,565.

SilverScript, released as v1 in September, lets developers write these rules in readable code. What covenants can’t do yet: many users changing one shared app state at once. That’s the goal of vProgs, still a prototype.

Next: apps checked by maths

Covenants guard one coin at a time. The next step is apps that many people share, like an exchange. They’d run off the network, and send Kaspa a : a few hundred bytes of maths showing the work was done right. Every node checks the proof in milliseconds instead of redoing the work.
